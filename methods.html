How the numbers are made

Plain-language notes on every number in the tracker: where it comes from, how it is added up, and what it can and cannot be compared with.

What partner data are

Every shipment that crosses a border is recorded twice: once by the country it leaves, as an export, and once by the country it enters, as an import. Both records are sent to UN Comtrade, the United Nations trade database.

Kiribati's own customs returns are the official record. "Partner-reported" data are the other side of the same shipments: what Thailand recorded as imports from Kiribati, what Australia recorded as exports to Kiribati, and so on. Economists call this "mirror" data.

The two records of one shipment differ even when both are right. Importers add freight and insurance to the value. The two sides can book the shipment in different months, under different product codes, or to different partners (for example when goods pass through a third country, or when fish is coded to the flag of the vessel that caught it).

The partner panel

The tracker watches a fixed list of partners, called the partner panel. A partner joins the panel if it accounted for at least half of one percent of Kiribati's partner-reported exports or imports over the reference window (2023 to 2025). Sixteen main regional and global partners are always included.

Each partner carries two weights: its share of partner-reported Kiribati exports and its share of partner-reported Kiribati imports over the same window. The panel and the weights are published in tracker/panel.json.

Keeping the panel fixed means that a change in a total reflects a change in trade, not a change in who is being counted.

Percent complete: count-based and trade-weighted

For a month, the count-based percent complete is the number of panel partners that have published that month, divided by the number of partners in the panel. For a quarter, it is the number of partner-months published divided by the number possible (three per partner). For a year, it is based on annual datasets where they exist and on twelve monthly datasets otherwise.

The trade-weighted version counts each partner by its weight. A month in which Thailand has published scores high on the export side even if several small partners have not, because Thailand buys most of the tuna.

A quarter that has not ended still counts three months as possible, so the reader can watch it fill up. Periods below 60 percent complete are drawn lighter and marked as preliminary.

Publication lags

Partner data are usable about four months after the reference month. Some large partners publish much later: China's latest monthly data are for December 2024 (annual only since), Korea's for December 2025, and Singapore's for March 2026. Thailand, the main tuna buyer, publishes within about four months.

Kiribati's own monthly returns are missing for 2019, 2021, January 2022 to May 2023, July and September 2024, and March and October 2025. Where Kiribati's figure is not published, the line on the charts has a gap; it is never drawn as zero.

How products are grouped

Goods are classified with the Harmonized System (HS), an international numbering scheme. Six-digit codes (HS6) are the finest level shared by all countries; four-digit codes (HS4) name product groups.

Tuna
HS6 030341 to 030349 (frozen tunas), 030487 (frozen tuna fillets) and 160414 (prepared or preserved tuna).
Fish and seafood
HS chapter 03 plus heading 1604.
Copra oil
HS6 151311 and 151319 (coconut oil).
Ships and aircraft
HS chapters 88 and 89.
Fuel
HS chapter 27.
Food
HS chapters 01 to 24, excluding chapter 03.

What is and is not comparable

Years in which Kiribati published all twelve months can be compared directly with the official balance-of-payments goods lines. For years with missing months, the customs figure is also shown scaled to twelve months; that is an estimate, and it is labelled as one.

Partner-reported exports are nearly complete: 96 to 99 percent of Kiribati's exports go to partners that publish promptly. Partner-reported imports are partial: partners record roughly 30 to 50 percent of what Kiribati records in most categories, and about 8 percent for fuel.

Comparisons at the exact month, partner and product level create false alarms. About three-quarters of apparent mismatches disappear when the same product is compared across all partners, or by quarter instead of by month. Read product comparisons for full years.

Differences are quoted in US dollars. Ratios (partner figure divided by Kiribati figure) are read only for full years, because a monthly ratio balloons when the figure in the denominator is close to zero.

Why no trade balance is shown

A trade balance is exports minus imports. Because partner data cover only part of Kiribati's imports, a balance built from them would be misleading. The tracker never computes one.

How the two sources are compared

Partner-reported exports are the sum of what each partner recorded as imports from Kiribati; partner-reported imports are the sum of what each partner recorded as exports to Kiribati. Kiribati-reported figures are Kiribati's own returns as published on Comtrade, in the same months. Both are shown in millions of US dollars to one decimal place. A blank means not published, never zero.

Ships and aircraft are rare, very large items, so every total is shown with and without them.

How the daily update works

A scheduled job runs once a day, asks UN Comtrade for any newly published partner data, rebuilds the data files and republishes the page. The time of the last run and the latest period covered are shown at the top of the tracker. If Comtrade cannot be reached, the previous data files stay in place.

Fleet activity (AIS): how it is made

The Fleet activity page adds a second, faster view of the tuna fleet: where Kiribati-flagged vessels were seen fishing and which ports they called at. This section explains where those numbers come from and what they cannot tell you.

Where the signals come from

Most large ships broadcast their identity and position using AIS (Automatic Identification System). Global Fishing Watch receives these broadcasts and publishes detections through its public interfaces. The tracker uses three kinds of detection from the Events API (version 3): fishing events, port visits, and encounters, which are two vessels seen close together at sea for long enough to transfer cargo. Vessel identities come from the Global Fishing Watch Vessels API.

Which vessels are counted

The list of vessels comes from the Western and Central Pacific Fisheries Commission (WCPFC) Record of Fishing Vessels, for the Kiribati, Nauru and Tuvalu registers. Each register entry is matched to a Global Fishing Watch identity first by radio call sign, then by IMO number. In the 20 October 2026 build, 116 register entries were current and 83 were matched to an identity with AIS history. "Kiribati-flagged" means the vessel's flag as reported by Global Fishing Watch, or, where that is not one of the three flags, the flag on the WCPFC register.

A flag says where a ship is registered. It does not say where its operator is based. The tracker makes no statement about ownership or residence.

Registration windows

A vessel's identity in Global Fishing Watch covers the whole life of the hull, including years before it joined the Kiribati register or after it left. Counting all of it would credit the flag with fishing it did under other flags. Each event is therefore kept only if it falls within a period when the vessel's own AIS reported the Kiribati, Nauru or Tuvalu flag. Where that is not available, the authorisation dates on the WCPFC register are used instead. This trimmed about 64,000 hours of earlier history, mostly in Russian waters, and only 145 hours (0.2%) inside Kiribati's waters.

What fishing hours measure, and what they do not

Fishing hours are the time during which a vessel's movement pattern looks like fishing. They measure effort. They do not measure fish caught: in a poor month a boat may fish longer for less. AIS reception also improves over time and differs by sea area, so part of a rise in hours can be better detection rather than more fishing. Vessels that switch off AIS are not seen.

What port calls measure, and what they do not

A port call is a visit detected from AIS, assigned to the country in which the port lies. A call does not mean that fish was unloaded, and the count says nothing about tonnage. The category "Other countries" includes ports whose country could not be identified.

At-sea transfers and the Thai port call

For each encounter between a fishing vessel and a carrier, the tracker follows the carrier's next port calls and notes the first call at a Thai port within 120 days. The share of transfers followed by such a call, and the median number of days between the transfer and that call, are reported on the Fleet page. They show where carriers went next. They do not show what a particular carrier held, and they are not a measure of the time between a catch and a customs record.

Why no estimate of tuna value is drawn from the hours

The tracker tested whether monthly fishing hours of Kiribati-flagged vessels predict the monthly value of tuna that importers record as coming from Kiribati. They do not. In levels the two series trend in opposite directions, so the relationship is dominated by trend. In 12-month changes, which remove trend and season, the fit is negligible (R² about 0.02). Hours alone therefore do not predict monthly value; a usable estimate would also need catch per hour and the tuna price. The Fleet page shows the two series side by side and does not draw an estimate from either.

Months in which fewer than three partners have published fish data are drawn lighter and are kept out of any such comparison. The 2026 partner figures are provisional and will be revised as partners publish.

Freshness and refresh

The stamp at the top of the Fleet page shows the last day covered by the AIS data and the date of the register export. The register has to be exported by hand from the WCPFC website, so it is refreshed about monthly; an older register export lowers coverage of new vessels but does not break the page.
