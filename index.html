Two correct records of different things: the first counts tuna by the flag of the catching vessel; the second counts all goods sold by Kiribati residents. The tracker estimates the tuna part of the second.

The question

Every shipment is recorded twice: by the country it leaves and by the country it enters. Kiribati's own records are the official ones. This tracker reads the other record, what Kiribati's trading partners declared to UN Comtrade (the United Nations trade database), to read Kiribati's tuna trade before the official figures are published.

For tuna the two records differ by a large multiple, because they answer different questions. Importers credit a fish to the flag of the catching vessel. The balance of payments (the official account of a country's dealings with the rest of the world) credits a sale to the country where the vessel's operator is resident. A purse seiner on the Kiribati register but run from abroad is "Kiribati" tuna in the first record and none in the second. The public vessel register gives a usable owner address for fewer than half of the vessels on the Kiribati, Nauru and Tuvalu flags, so neither record settles how much of the catch belongs to Kiribati residents.

The question is therefore: what is the true value of the tuna that Kiribati residents sell abroad? The Kiribati National Statistics Office measures it under the balance-of-payments rule, inside its goods-exports line, from the returns it receives; it does not publish a tuna line. The tracker offers a second reading of the same quantity from different evidence, to help that work: five inputs, and thirty AI agents who reason from them to an estimate one layer at a time. The page follows that order: the answer so far, the path of a fish, the five inputs, the inference, and how it will be tested.

The answer so far

Three records of the same tuna, by year. The estimate is the middle line; its band is where the thirty agents' answers fall.

How a fish becomes a statistic

Follow one tuna from the sea to the published figures. No single source sees the whole path; the marks show what the tracker can and cannot observe at each step. Steps follow the fish; the six layers of the inference are the arithmetic from the partner figure to the estimate.

Seen by ship-tracking (AIS), Global Fishing Watch Recorded in official trade or customs data Not observed by the tracker
  1. 1

    Catch

    Purse-seine and longline vessels on the Kiribati register fish in Kiribati's waters and in neighbouring Pacific waters.

    • Fishing hours. Hours spent fishing, from AIS. This is effort, not catch.
    • Vessel register. The WCPFC register says which vessels are on the Kiribati flag.
    • Not observed: tonnes caught.
  2. 2

    Hand-over at sea

    The catcher stays on the fishing grounds. It passes its catch to a refrigerated carrier vessel that comes alongside.

    • Meetings at sea. Vessel pairs seen together, from AIS.
    • Not observed: what is transferred, or how much.
  3. 3

    Carrier to port

    The carrier sails to a port, often Bangkok, and unloads. The catcher itself usually does not make this trip.

    • Port visits. Carrier calls, from AIS.
    • Not observed: tonnage unloaded, or whether it is the same fish.
  4. 4

    Import record

    The importer's customs records the arrival. Under its rules of origin, fish caught at sea is credited to the country whose flag the catching vessel flies.

    • Partner-reported imports in UN Comtrade: USD value of tuna "from Kiribati". This is the main tracker.
    • Timing: published about four months after the month.
  5. 5

    Kiribati's own books

    Kiribati records its own exports. Official statistics are the reference.

    • Kiribati customs returns in UN Comtrade: published late and with gaps.
    • Balance of payments (IMF): goods exports, annual.

Two ways of recording the same fish

Trade statistics (step 4) ask which country's vessel caught the fish, and credit that country as the origin.

The balance of payments (step 5) asks who owns the fish when it is sold abroad. If the operator is resident in Kiribati, the sale is a Kiribati export; if the operator is resident elsewhere, it is not.

These are different questions, so the two records can differ without either one being wrong. The tracker does not say which applies to any vessel.

What the inputs add to one another

The AIS steps (1 to 3) arrive within days, the import record (step 4) about four months later, the official figures (step 5) later still. Together they show where activity is before the trade figures are in, and which steps nobody measures. The inference starts from step 4 and reasons toward the quantity that step 5 records: the sales of resident operators.

AI-based inference: from the partner figure to true exports

Thirty AI agents (the "experts") receive the same evidence and instructions and work alone. Each agent is one fresh session of a large language model, with no memory of the other sessions. Each writes its principles, walks from the raw partner figure to its own estimate in six layers, then checks itself. A program combines the thirty answers. The spread across agents is the room for judgment the evidence leaves; it is not a standard error.

The full detail, the six layers with each agent's reasons, the principles the agents wrote and how they used the official figures, is on the Inference page. The method is in Methods.

Sources. UN Comtrade (partner returns and Kiribati's own returns); IMF balance-of-payments statistics; Global Fishing Watch; WCPFC Record of Fishing Vessels; the statistical standards and research listed on the Rules and research page.
