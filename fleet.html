What this page shows

Most large ships carry a transponder that broadcasts the ship's identity and position, called AIS (Automatic Identification System). Global Fishing Watch collects these signals and uses them to detect when a vessel is fishing, when it meets another vessel at sea, and when it visits a port.

This page counts those detections for vessels that were on the Kiribati flag, and only for the period each vessel was registered under it. The diagram below follows one tuna from the sea to the statistics and marks where each step is, and is not, measured. The charts then show how many hours the fleet was seen fishing, how those hours sit beside the tuna that importers record as coming from Kiribati, and which countries' ports the catching vessels called at.

Every number here is an observation of where vessels were. None is an estimate of how much fish was caught, sold or earned.

Words used on this page
Kiribati-flagged vessel
A vessel entered on the Kiribati register of fishing vessels. A flag says where a ship is registered. It does not say where its operator is based or where its profits go.
Fishing hours
Time during which AIS signals show a vessel behaving as it does when fishing. Hours measure effort, not fish caught.
Port call
A visit to a port detected from AIS. A call does not mean fish was unloaded.
At-sea transfer
Two vessels detected close together for long enough to transfer cargo, typically a fishing vessel and a carrier. Also called a transhipment.
Partner-reported
What importing countries record in UN Comtrade about goods they bring in from Kiribati. See the main tracker.

How a fish becomes a statistic

Follow one tuna from the sea to the published figures. Each step leaves a trace somewhere, but no single source sees the whole path. The marks show what the tracker can and cannot observe at each step.

Seen by ship-tracking (AIS), Global Fishing Watch Recorded in official trade or customs data Not observed by the tracker
  1. 1

    Catch

    Purse-seine and longline vessels on the Kiribati register fish in Kiribati's waters and in neighbouring Pacific waters.

    • Fishing hours. Hours spent fishing, from AIS. This is effort, not catch.
    • Vessel register. The WCPFC register says which vessels are on the Kiribati flag.
    • Not observed: tonnes caught.
  2. 2

    Hand-over at sea

    The catcher stays on the fishing grounds. It passes its catch to a refrigerated carrier vessel that comes alongside.

    • Meetings at sea. Vessel pairs seen together, from AIS.
    • Not observed: what is transferred, or how much.
  3. 3

    Carrier to port

    The carrier sails to a port, often Bangkok, and unloads. The catcher itself usually does not make this trip.

    • Port visits. Carrier calls, from AIS.
    • Not observed: tonnage unloaded, or whether it is the same fish.
  4. 4

    Import record

    The importer's customs records the arrival. Under its rules of origin, fish caught at sea is credited to the country whose flag the catching vessel flies.

    • Partner-reported imports in UN Comtrade: USD value of tuna "from Kiribati". This is the main tracker.
    • Timing: published about four months after the month.
  5. 5

    Kiribati's own books

    Kiribati records its own exports. Official statistics are the reference.

    • Kiribati customs returns in UN Comtrade: published late and with gaps.
    • Balance of payments (IMF): goods exports, annual.

Two ways of recording the same fish

Trade statistics (step 4) ask which country's vessel caught the fish, and credit that country as the origin.

The balance of payments (step 5) asks who owns the fish when it is sold abroad. If the operator is resident in Kiribati, the sale is a Kiribati export; if the operator is resident elsewhere, it is not.

These are different questions, so the two records can differ without either one being wrong. The tracker does not say which applies to any vessel.

What this tracker adds

The AIS steps (1 to 3) arrive within days. The import record (step 4) arrives months later. Reading them together shows where activity is happening long before the trade figures are in, and it shows which steps have no measurement at all.

The AIS steps describe where vessels were. They do not estimate catch, value or any official statistic.

What this page does not show

  • It shows no vessel or company names, and no individual ship's track. Only totals are published.
  • It makes no statement about who owns or operates the vessels. A flag is not residence: whether an operator is resident in Kiribati or abroad is a separate question that this page does not address.
  • It does not estimate catch, exports or earnings from the AIS signals. Official Kiribati statistics remain the reference for those.
  • It does not compare AIS activity with any fees or government revenue.

How these numbers are made, and what they can and cannot measure, is explained in How the numbers are made.
